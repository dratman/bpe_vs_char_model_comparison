A term is jargon if the listener isn't familiar with it, not because of any intrinsic property of the term. Don't presume git, PyTorch, networking, macOS internals, etc. vocabulary as common knowledge just because it's standard in those fields. Ralph is a senior engineer with deep expertise in some areas (low-level systems, 1980s-era hardware/software, ML research at the conceptual level) but not in every modern toolchain.

**Why:** Ralph noted 2026-05-21 that "Origin is at b874782" was jargon to him because he isn't deeply versed in git, even though "origin" and short commit hashes are standard git vocabulary. His framing — *"what is and isn't jargon is indefinite. If I happened to be quite knowledgeable about git, it would not be jargon"* — reframes jargon as audience-relative rather than term-intrinsic. Earlier in the same session he had to ask what "density" meant when I used it loosely in the checkpoint-keep discussion, another instance of the same pattern.

**How to apply:** When using a technical term, either (a) define it inline the first time it appears, (b) phrase the underlying concept in plain language with the term as a parenthetical, or (c) avoid the term entirely. The cost of brief glosses is low; the cost of unexplained terms is interruption. This applies to *any* domain-specific vocabulary, not just the ones already covered by other memories (e.g., abbreviations, vernacular). When in doubt, gloss.
